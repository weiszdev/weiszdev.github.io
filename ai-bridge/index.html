Recognition

Seven situations

Not problems in the abstract — specific afternoons that happen in consumer product companies. Read these before the numbers. If you have lived three of them, the rest of this page is just arithmetic.

Situation 01

You have designed this part before.

An engineer spends two days modeling a bracket. It already exists in the vault — designed eighteen months ago, saved under a name that made sense to whoever saved it, findable by nobody since. Two weeks later the same thing happens with a fastener callout.

Meanwhile, somewhere else, a supplier is building to a drawing two revisions behind, and you will find out when the pallet arrives with the old geometry on it.

What it costsFull engineering rate to recreate assets you already own — and every duplicate part number carries tooling, inventory and sustaining cost for the rest of its life. Plus the scrap run nobody budgeted.
→ Pillar 01 — The Product Data Spine
Situation 02

You found out from a review.

A one-star review says the adjustment knob strips out after a month. Somebody forwards it around. Engineering asks how widespread it is, and the honest answer is that nobody knows — you would have to read the tickets.

Eventually someone does, and finds four hundred of them. The first one came in during week two. You are now in month seven, three production runs deep, with the same knob in every unit on a Costco floor.

What it costsEvery unit built between week two and month seven carries a defect you already had the data to catch. Warranty, returns, review damage on the listing, and a retailer conversation you did not want to have.
→ Pillar 02 — The Quality Signal Loop
Situation 03

Overstocked and sold out. Same week.

The warehouse is full of last year's colorway, which is not moving and will be marked down. At the same time the hero SKU has been dark for six weeks in the middle of Q4, and the club buyer noticed before you did.

The forecast that produced both outcomes was one spreadsheet trying to serve two channels that behave nothing alike — enormous lumpy club orders and smooth small DTC velocity, averaged into a single number that describes neither.

What it costsCash frozen in inventory that will sell at a discount if it sells at all, and margin left on the table in the one quarter that matters. Obsolescence is the largest single component of carrying cost for a model-year product.
→ Pillar 03 — Demand & Inventory Intelligence
Situation 04

The remittance came in light again.

Forty thousand dollars short. There is a deduction code. Somebody could figure out what happened — an ASN that went out late, a label that failed inspection, a pallet built to the wrong spec — but tracing it takes a day and there are nine more like it this month.

So finance books it as cost of doing business. Nobody disputes the invalid ones. Nobody routes the valid ones back to the process that caused them, so next quarter it happens again, and the quarter after that.

What it costsWalmart charges 3% of COGS on OTIF failures. Target charges 5% with a $150 minimum. Costco publishes nothing and assesses case by case — which means you cannot even forecast it. The recurring version of this is the expensive part.
→ Pillar 04 — Retail Compliance & Deduction Defense
Situation 05

The discount window closed while the invoice sat.

A supplier calls asking about payment. Your AP lead spends twenty minutes finding out that the invoice has been in an exception queue for nine days because a line item did not match the PO by four dollars.

The 2% early-payment discount expired on day eleven. This happens often enough that nobody flags it anymore.

What it costsThe industry average is $9.84 to process one invoice; the top performers do it for $2.65. Roughly a fifth of AP's time goes to answering supplier inquiries about invoices they cannot find. This is the most reliably fixable line in the business.
→ Pillar 05 — Order-to-Cash and Procure-to-Pay
Situation 06

You are shooting the same product for the fourth time.

New colorway, same product. Full production cycle — studio, lighting, photographer, retouching. Then the retailer updates their content template and someone retypes every spec by hand into a new format, for every SKU, from a spreadsheet that may or may not match what engineering last released.

Two of the dimensions on the live listing are wrong. They have been wrong for a year.

What it costsProduction spend that scales linearly with SKU count when it should not, launch dates gated by a photo shoot, and listing errors that drive returns and support contacts — which loops straight back to Situation 02.
→ Pillar 06 — Commercial Content & Media Efficiency
Situation 07

The seats renewed and nobody noticed.

You bought AI licenses last year. There was a kickoff. A few people were excited. Twelve months later the renewal comes through finance and someone asks what it did for us — and the room is quiet, because the honest answer is that three people use it to rewrite emails.

Nobody was trained to be inventive with it. Nobody owned a number. So usage collapsed to whatever the vendor demoed on day one, and the tool became a line item instead of a capability.

What it costsThe license fee is the small part. The real cost is a year of compounding advantage handed to the third of manufacturers who wired this into how the business actually runs — plus an organization that now believes AI does not work here.
→ Pillar 07 — Capability, Governance and the Innovation Habit

None of these are technology failures. Every one of them is a system that already holds the answer, and nobody with the time to go get it.

Where mid-market manufacturers actually sit

BCG's July 2026 survey of 152 CEOs at companies above $500M found 89% seeing cost or revenue benefit in targeted areas — but only 26% embedding AI into broader business transformation, and 65% still running pilots. Roughly 5% have reached transformative bottom-line impact at scale.

In the middle market the split is sharper. RSM's March 2026 survey (n=129 manufacturers) found 88% with AI at least partially integrated but only 32% fully integrated across core operations. A separate Kaufman Rossin study of mid-sized manufacturers found none fully operationalized, with 73% still in early preparation stages — a small sample, so read it as direction rather than fact.

The honest read: the market is not split into adopters and holdouts. It is splitting into a third who have wired AI into how the business actually runs and two-thirds who bought licenses. Being in the second group is not a technology problem. It is a systems, data and training problem — which is exactly the problem this plan solves.

Why the pilots stall

MIT's NANDA initiative reported in 2025 that 95% of generative-AI pilots produced no measurable P&L impact, and that AI sourced from specialist partners succeeded roughly twice as often as AI built in-house (~67% vs ~33%).

Handle this number carefully. It is the most-quoted AI statistic in existence and also the most-attacked — the methodology was widely criticized and the paper was never peer-reviewed. I cite it because BCG's independent finding explains it: 89% of executives claim benefits while only 14% define P&L impact. The failure isn't the model. It's that nobody defined what winning looked like, wired it to a number, or trained anyone to run it.

The Plan

Seven pillars

Each pillar names the failure mode, the intervention, the evidence behind it, and the modeled dollar effect. Where the evidence does not exist, it says so.

Pillar 01

The Product Data Spine

PDM · PLM · SOLIDWORKS · Engineering
Failure

Engineers cannot find what already exists, so they rebuild it. Revisions live in three places. The BOM the factory builds from and the BOM engineering thinks is current are not the same document. Every change is a manual chase.

Evidence

Tech-Clarity's 2024 study (n=245) found engineers lose 19% of their time — one full day a week — to non-value-added data management, with only about half of engineering time going to actual design. Their earlier breakdown attributes the largest slices to searching for information (23%) and recreating data that couldn't be found (12%), with 20% of time spent working from outdated information.

Intervention

Clean the spine before layering intelligence on it. Enforced revision control, one authoritative BOM, structured part attributes and a real reuse taxonomy. Then AI-assisted retrieval over the corrected library — semantic part search, spec drafting, change-impact summaries.

$213,000/yr of engineering payroll is currently consumed by data friction — 3,040 hours across 8 engineers. A one-third recovery returns ~$71,000/yr and ~1,000 engineering hours to actual product work. Assumes 8 FTE × 2,000 hrs × 19% (Tech-Clarity 2024) × $70/hr loaded. The one-third recovery rate is a target I will commit to, not a published benchmark.
Pillar 02

The Quality Signal Loop

Customer Service → Engineering · The Bridge
"Maybe you could also help with building better quality data reports from AI to see our product issues and know what things to attack. Our CS manager does it but doesn't have time to really focus on it. That alone would be another huge project." — From an operator conversation
Failure

The company already owns the most valuable product-quality dataset it will ever have: every support ticket, warranty claim, return reason, parts request and review. It sits in a helpdesk, unread at scale, because one manager cannot hand-read 30,000 contacts a year. The defect signal exists. Nobody has time to hear it.

Why it's the bridge

This is the pillar that connects the two halves of the business. CS text is the only continuous, unfiltered feedback loop from the field back to engineering. Classify it, cluster it, tie it to SKU, lot, revision and channel — and the ERP's warranty cost lines, the PLM's change queue and the DTC review score all start pointing at the same root cause. Without it, PDM and ERP are two systems that never speak.

Intervention

A standing pipeline: auto-classify every contact into failure taxonomy, cluster emerging issues, rank by units affected × warranty cost × review damage, and push a weekly one-page "what to attack" report to engineering and quality. The CS manager stops being the bottleneck and becomes the reviewer. Deflection of routine contacts is the second-order benefit, not the headline.

~$88,000/yr in support cost, plus the defect-detection window shortening from quarters to weeks. Modeled at 45% AI resolution of 30,000 contacts, at $6.50 saved per resolved contact. Cost per human contact $6–12 and per AI resolution $0.99–2.00 are Intercom/Fin.ai figures — a vendor with a product to sell. Independent testing puts resolution rates at 45–53% against Intercom's claimed 76%, so this models 45%, not 76%. The larger value — catching a defect in week 3 instead of month 7 — is real but I will not put a fabricated number on it.
Pillar 03

Demand & Inventory Intelligence

NetSuite · Planning · Working Capital
Failure

Forecasting runs on spreadsheets and last year plus a percentage. Club-channel orders arrive lumpy and enormous; DTC is smooth and small; one forecast tries to serve both. The result is simultaneous overstock and stockout — cash frozen in slow SKUs while the hero SKU goes dark in Q4.

Evidence

McKinsey's 2024 CPG transformation work documents a named case at +13% forecast accuracy, −40% product shortages and −35% inventory. e2open's 2024 benchmark study (network data 2019–2023) found demand sensing cut forecast error by about a third, and reported that 85% of items are slow movers — precisely the population where statistical baselines degrade worst.

Intervention

Separate the two demand signals. Feed the club channel from POS and program calendars; feed DTC from its own velocity and media plan. NetSuite Predictive Planning and multivariate forecasting where they fit; a purpose-built model where they don't. Then attack the obsolescence tail, which is the largest single component of carrying cost for a model-year product.

~$525,000/yr in carrying cost avoided, plus ~$2.1M of working capital released one time. Modeled at a 15% inventory reduction on $14M, at a 25% annual carrying cost. Deliberately conservative: 15%, not McKinsey's 35%. The 20–30% carrying-cost benchmark is the standard planning assumption but traces to Richardson (1995) — everyone recycles it, including NetSuite's own documentation. Treat it as a convention, not fresh research.
Pillar 04

Retail Compliance & Deduction Defense

EDI · Club Channel · Costco & Big-Box
Failure

Chargebacks land as line items nobody owns. An ASN goes out late, a label fails inspection, a pallet is built wrong — and the penalty is netted off the remittance months later, unexamined. Finance books it as cost of doing business. Nobody traces it back to the process that caused it.

Evidence

The published fee schedules are unambiguous. Walmart enforces 98% OTIF with a 3% of COGS penalty on non-compliant orders. Target's revised policy (effective Aug 2024) charges 5% of COGS with a $150 minimum on on-time fill rate failures, and 3% of COGS for ASN violations. Walmart's SQEP program adds flat fees — $200 per defective PO plus $1 per case for document inaccuracies.

On Costco specifically

Costco publishes no OTIF threshold and no standardized penalty percentage. Chargebacks are assessed case-by-case per violation, and their EDI specifications are proprietary and diverge from industry standard — with the 856 ASN the critical transaction. Any Costco chargeback figure you have been quoted is unsourced, because the source does not exist publicly. What is defensible is the process discipline, not a benchmark number.

Intervention

Instrument the EDI stack so every 850 → 856 → 810 cycle is monitored, not just transmitted. Auto-validate ASNs before send. Then run deduction forensics: classify every chargeback by root cause, auto-draft dispute packets for the invalid ones, and route the valid ones back to the operational process that caused them.

$100,000–$290,000/yr recovered or prevented on $48M of club-channel volume. This is the softest number on the page. Practitioner estimates (Jon Allen, Woodridge Retail Group, 2026) put deduction leak at 5–15% of gross sales with 10–20% invalid and another 20% preventable-but-unchallenged; SPS Commerce reports only 20–30% of deductions are ever disputed, with a 40% win-back rate on those that are. None of this is an audited industry benchmark — it is two practitioner sources, one of them a vendor. I model a 2% deduction rate and recover 10–30% of it. Your actual remittance detail replaces this range in week one.
Pillar 05

Order-to-Cash and Procure-to-Pay

NetSuite · AP · AR · Finance Ops
Failure

Invoices are keyed by hand, exceptions queue up behind one person, and early-payment discounts expire unclaimed. It is unglamorous and it is the most reliably fixable line in the business.

Evidence

Ardent Partners' State of ePayables 2025 (n=204, data collected March–May 2025) is the strongest dataset on this page: average cost to process one invoice $9.84 against a best-in-class $2.65; average processing time 8.2 days versus 2.9; exception rate 18.4% versus 11.1%. AP staff spend 21.9% of their time answering supplier inquiries. 44% of AP teams already use AI, with another 34% planning to inside twelve months.

Intervention

NetSuite Bill Capture plus document intelligence for extraction; exception triage routed by predicted cause rather than by queue order; a supplier-inquiry assistant against live AP data. Target the middle of the Ardent range, not best-in-class — a $4.50 cost per invoice is achievable without a transformation program.

~$96,000/yr on 18,000 invoices, moving from the $9.84 average toward $4.50 per invoice. Additional upside in early-payment discount capture at roughly 2% of invoice value where terms allow. Ardent is an independent analyst firm with a disclosed sample and a multi-year series; the report is sponsor-supported but the benchmark predates any single sponsor. This is the most defensible figure in the plan.
Pillar 06

Commercial Content & Media Efficiency

DTC · Ecommerce · Brand
Failure

Every SKU refresh triggers a full production cycle. Listing copy, A+ content, lifestyle imagery and video are rebuilt from scratch for each channel, each retailer template, each variant. Meanwhile paid media runs on manual campaign structures that leave measurable efficiency on the table.

Evidence

Unilever reported in 2025 that AI and digital-twin product shoots made content roughly 50% cheaper and 2× faster company-wide, with one business unit at −55% cost and 65% faster turnaround. Meta disclosed in its Q3 2025 earnings call that Advantage+ lead campaigns delivered 14% lower cost per lead on average, with $60B annualized revenue running through its end-to-end AI ad tools.

Intervention

Build a product-content pipeline off the PDM spine from Pillar 01 — spec data flows into listing copy, variant matrices and retailer templates automatically instead of being retyped. Digital-twin imagery for variant and colorway shots. Restructure paid media around automated campaign types with clean conversion signal.

~$470,000/yr — roughly $320,000 from a 10% efficiency gain on $3.2M of DTC media, and $150,000 from a 30% reduction on $500K of content production. Both source figures are self-reported by parties with an interest: Unilever is publicizing agency savings, Meta is measuring its own ad products. I therefore model 10% media efficiency against Meta's claimed 14%, and 30% content savings against Unilever's claimed 50%. Efficiency gains in auction-based media also partly get bid away — Meta's own price per ad rose 10% year over year.
Pillar 07

Capability, Governance and the Innovation Habit

People · Training · The Multiplier
Failure

Tools get bought. Nobody is trained to be inventive with them, so usage collapses to whatever the vendor demoed. Six months later the license renews and no one can point to a number that moved. This is the pillar that decides whether the other six survive contact with reality.

Evidence

BCG's 2026 CEO survey found 65% of companies still running pilots and only 14% defining P&L impact for all AI initiatives — with more than half naming the missing AI-to-P&L link as a top barrier. MIT NANDA's finding that externally-partnered AI succeeded roughly twice as often as internally-built AI points the same direction: the constraint is operating discipline, not model capability.

Intervention

Every pillar ships with a named owner, a baseline measurement taken before anything changes, and a single metric wired to the P&L. Working sessions with the people who do the job — the CS manager, the planner, the AP lead, the design engineer — building prompts and workflows against their own live data, not a training sandbox. The deliverable is a team that keeps finding the next one after I leave.

The multiplier on the other six. Not a separate savings line — the difference between capturing the model and joining the 65% still piloting. I will not attach a fabricated dollar figure to training ROI. There is no credible benchmark for it and anyone who quotes you one is selling something.

The cost of not moving

The number that matters is not the upside. It is that the leak is already running. Every one of these losses is being paid right now, monthly, out of margin — booked as payroll, as carrying cost, as remittance deductions, as agency invoices. Nothing needs to go wrong for the money to disappear. It disappears by default.

Three years of standing still costs roughly $4.04M in recurring value, plus the working capital that stays frozen, plus a widening capability gap against the 32% of manufacturers who already have AI fully integrated into core operations. And the pilot tax is real: money spent on tools nobody was trained to use is spent either way — the only question is whether it produces a number.

To be precise about what this claim is: this is a model, not a measurement. The benchmarks are sourced and cited. The assumptions are stated. The arithmetic is auditable. What it is not is a promise — the first thirty days of any engagement exist to replace these assumptions with your actual numbers, and the model may well come down.

Diligence

What I will not tell you

A consultant who has never met a number they couldn't find is a consultant who is inventing them. Here is everything I went looking for and could not source.

  • Any quantified AI improvement in PLM or PDM. Part reuse, BOM accuracy, design search speed, engineering change cycle time, spec generation — there is no independent, methodologically disclosed study. An independent PLM analyst review in November 2025 found vendors stating benefits qualitatively only, with zero substantiated percentage improvements. If someone quotes you "AI improved part reuse by 40%," ask for the methodology. There isn't one.
  • The average cost of an engineering change order. APQC maintains formal benchmarks for both ECO cost and ECO cycle time, but both are behind membership paywalls. Every dollar-per-ECO figure circulating publicly traces to vendor marketing with no underlying study. This one is purchasable if it becomes load-bearing.
  • The cost of an EDI error. No defensible figure exists. What is defensible is the published retailer fee schedule — Walmart's $200 per PO plus $1 per case for a bad ASN is a real, citable number. The generic "EDI errors cost $X" is not.
  • Costco's chargeback schedule or OTIF threshold. Costco publishes neither. Any number you have been given is unsourced.
  • Quantified AI outcomes from NetSuite customers. I searched the product pages, the 2025.2 release notes and SuiteWorld 2025 press materials. There is one qualitative testimonial and no percentages. Also note: NetSuite Next, the agentic platform announced October 2025, was North-America-only "within the next twelve months" — verify current availability before planning around it.
  • AI-driven return-rate reduction for durable goods. Every claim traces to vendor blogs, and the underlying research is apparel-sizing work that does not transfer to fitness equipment, where returns are driven by transit damage, assembly difficulty and buyer's remorse.
  • Any credible ROI benchmark for AI training itself. Doesn't exist. Pillar 07 carries no dollar figure for exactly that reason.

How it runs

Ninety days, sprint-based

The shape

  1. Days 1–15 — Baseline. Pull real numbers: remittance detail, AP volume and cost, inventory and turns by SKU class, engineering time survey, twelve months of CS contacts. Every assumption above gets replaced or confirmed. Deliverable: the same model, populated with your data.
  2. Days 16–30 — Sequence. Rank the seven pillars by value ÷ effort against what we found. Some will collapse. One or two will be bigger than modeled. Pick the two that ship first.
  3. Days 31–75 — Build. Working sprints on the top pillars, with the operators who own them. Shipped artifacts, not recommendations — the weekly quality report actually running, the ASN validation actually firing, the deduction dispute packets actually going out.
  4. Days 76–90 — Measure and hand off. Before-and-after against the day-15 baseline. Owners named. Runbooks written. Mutual evaluation on scope and rate for what comes next.

Why this profile fits this problem

Mechanical engineer by training — 3,000+ hours in SOLIDWORKS, one of 32 SOLIDWORKS Certified Experts in Minnesota, 3,500+ technical support cases and 100+ engineers trained as an application engineer. Product development at a consumer fitness manufacturer, including co-designing an industry-first seated elliptical. Then engineering management, then building and shipping eleven production web products across seven industries in a single year using AI tooling end to end.

That combination is the point. Most fractional consultants bring strategy or domain depth, not both, and almost none can build. The PDM side of this plan is not theoretical to me — I lived in that system, trained the people who used it, and watched exactly how the data spine breaks. The AI side is not theoretical either; it is what I ship with daily.

Rate: $9,500/month, 90-day initial term, value-based rather than hourly. Covers up to three defined business lines. Either party may exit with 14 days' written notice after day 30. All work product transfers to the client on payment.
